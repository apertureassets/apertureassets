Destination

Dolomites

8 days · £1,900

I travelled in September, just after the August school holiday crowds have cleared and slightly cheaper as a result. The weather held up well throughout, only one afternoon brought any rain, and I largely dodged the poorer conditions that tended to roll through overnight. I flew from Stansted to Venice Marco Polo and picked up a rental car, basing myself in Corvara for 3 nights before moving on to Rasun Anterselva for 4 nights.

Travel Notes

Before You Fly

UK passport holders don't need a visa for Italy. Depending on the hire company, it's worth picking up an International Driving Permit before you travel, around £6 and valid for a couple of years, cheap enough that it's worth having just in case.

I flew Ryanair return for £230, including a checked bag, an under-seat bag and a larger cabin bag, the cheapest option I could find for the dates. Milan is also an option and covers a similar distance to the Dolomites, but Venice is the nearer airport. My outbound flight left at 9:05am and the return was at 10:45pm.

My phone plan includes EU roaming, so I didn't need an eSIM. If yours doesn't, Airalo is a solid option, £18 for unlimited data over 7 days.

Book car hire before you leave to lock in availability and the best rate.

Travel Notes

On The Ground Essentials

I booked through Discover Cars, with the listing provided by Last Minute Rent and the car itself supplied by Galdieri Rent Cars. The rental cost €265 for the trip period, including breakdown cover at around €30, which felt like a sensible add given the region. Separately I bought damage waiver protection through Simplyexcess for £15.99, significantly cheaper than Discover Cars' own offering. It's worth checking your rental company's requirements before you travel. A customer at the desk next to me was on the phone trying to find another company that would accept a debit card, having been refused at the counter for not having a credit card. Check the terms of your rental provider before booking to avoid any unnecessary hassle to your trip.

Fuel stations were fairly easy to find across the region, but tended to be cheaper in the main resort towns than the smaller villages, so it pays to time a fuel stop rather than filling up at the first station you pass. Some garages are self-service, others are attended, where staff fill the car for you. Fuel symbols are the same as the UK, E5 for petrol and B10 for diesel, so there's no language barrier to work through at the pump.

Toll stations generally accept card or cash, but carry both just in case one method has an issue at the booth. Toilets across the region are coin-only, running around €1 a visit, so keep loose change on you.

Connectivity was patchy in places, particularly around Prato Piazza and Tre Cime, so download routes or map locations before you travel rather than relying on a signal once you're there.

Speed cameras tend to sit in the smaller villages you pass through, usually around 50kph. Stick to the local limit regardless, even if the cars around you are pushing to go faster.

Itinerary

Corvara · 3 days

I stayed at Hotel Jägerhof in Corvara, in the Badia valley, for €475 half board. Dinner was a four-course affair each evening with a choice on the second and third courses, and the food was excellent throughout. The staff were genuinely friendly, and the waiting staff, Leo in particular, made the experience. The hotel was well placed, parking was included at no extra cost, and the balcony looked straight out onto the surrounding mountains. It was the typical alpine wooden lodge you picture when you think of a ski resort, with a modern twist running through it.

Day one was the drive from the airport to the hotel, around 3.5 hours, and it's worth building in some extra time given the mountain roads and switchbacks you'll be climbing and descending through on the way. The views are incredible, but you need to stay focused. Locals tend to cut corners into your lane, and you're driving on the opposite side to what you're used to, which takes some getting used to, especially with a bus appearing mid-corner. Take your time and you'll be fine, but some driving confidence or international experience will help. After checking in, I unpacked and settled in after an early flight and a long drive, then headed down for dinner.

Day two was Passo Pordoi, taking the cable car to the summit at around 2,850m. It's noticeably colder up top than the weather at the bottom suggests, which I found out the hard way having only brought a fleece, and by the end of the visit my hands had gone properly numb. The cable car cost €32 return, plus €7.50 for parking. This was the first time I'd properly stood on a summit with just a barrier between me and the edge, and it was a genuinely surreal moment, any nerves from the ride up wore off fast once the views opened up. You can hike up instead, but it's steep enough that good fitness and hiking poles are genuinely needed, and the walking tracks at the summit eventually take you away from the railing, so that sense of safety doesn't last the whole way round. The summit itself is like a plateau across the Dolomites, and the views were special, including a clear look at the glacier on Marmolada, the highest peak in the range. There's a rifugio at the top for food or drink, with meals running €20-€30. I spent around an hour and a half up there, and between the parking and the lift, this was probably the best value cable car of the trip.

In the afternoon I headed to Marmolada. The cable car cost €40 including parking, and there are two options, taking you two-thirds of the way or the full three stages. The full route brings you to Punta Rocca at 3,265m, just short of Punta Penia, the true summit at 3,343m, which is only reachable from there via a technical climbing route. The ride itself was nerve-wracking on the second and third sections, where the car swings slightly crossing a kink in the cable. It settles down quickly, but it's worth knowing about if you're uneasy with cable cars, as I was. You can view the glacier close up from a sectioned area, though the barrier is just netting, so I stuck to the sturdier platform instead. The glacier was great to see up close, though I'll admit I felt a little underwhelmed, having been spoilt by Franz Josef and Fox Glacier in New Zealand. The altitude made the stairs up from the glacier viewpoint a bit of a struggle, though there were only four flights, so it wasn't excessive.

The views at the top are a full 360 degrees, and there's a First World War museum at the second stop if you've got the time or interest, along with another rifugio for food and drink. It's cold and exposed up here too, so bring layers, especially if it's windy. I did Pordoi and Marmolada on the same day since they're close together and offer different perspectives, one across the wider Dolomites, the other right up against the glacier itself.

Day three was Seceda, the priciest cable car of the trip at €74, with a 5% discount for booking ahead, though I booked on the day to make sure I had good weather given the price. Parking cost €17.50, using the central underground car park, handy to know since there are a few lots with similar names and some cost more. Seceda sits at around 2,500m and the journey up runs in two sections. It gets busy, so arrive early if you want to avoid queuing. The first cable car was quick, but the second had a 10-20 minute wait. Given the cost, this isn't somewhere to arrive at for an hour, so I made it a full day, spending most of it walking and shooting different perspectives of the ridge.

From the top station it's around a 10-minute walk up to the ridgeline, uphill on gravel, so decent footwear matters. There are several good photo spots along the ridge, though the popular ones draw a queue of their own. Later in the day cloud rolled in around the surrounding peaks, which actually added a nice sense of depth to the shots I was getting. Walking the ridgeline itself costs another €5, a toll installed by a private landowner as a protest against overtourism, and given what you've already paid to get up there, I don't think it's worth it. The view is the ridgeline with the Geisler/Odle peaks behind it, which you can still appreciate without paying the extra. The hike overall paid off despite the price of the cable car, and bike hire is another option if you want a quicker way around the area.

From the summit I walked down to Troier Hütte for lunch, another €20-€30 item, then hiked back up rather than taking the lift, around 4 miles in total. The altitude made the climb tougher than the distance suggests, especially on the steeper sections.

Corvara Honest Take

Corvara was well located for everything I'd planned in this stretch of the trip. I did make one planning error though: skipping Cinque Torri, which is only around 45 minutes away. I'd recommend building it into this section of the itinerary before heading north. I really liked this region, and it worked out notably cheaper than Val Gardena and Ortisei, despite sitting slightly further from some of the stops and viewpoints. You're based within the Sella Ronda though, so nothing ends up being especially far. The hotel was worth the money, particularly the half-board addition, and I'd recommend booking it if it's available. The food was a mix of Italian and South Tyrolean dishes, all excellent.

On the subject of cable cars generally: they're expensive, and there's a fair argument they should cost less than in winter, given all you're paying for is access to the summit rather than a lift pass covering a full ski area. That said, all three I used across these two days delivered incredible views and paid off despite the cost. The alternative is hiking, which is realistic at both Passo Pordoi and Seceda if you've got the fitness and confidence for it. Marmolada's a different case: reaching Punta Rocca on foot isn't a walking option, it's a technical mountaineering route requiring proper climbing equipment, so the cable car is really the only way up for most visitors.

Itinerary

Rasun Anterselva · 4 days

I booked to stay at Hotel Brunnerhof in Anterselva, a stone's throw from the biathlon Winter Olympic stadium and the Austrian border. The hotel cost £370 for the 4 nights including half board, with the evening meal running to five courses, the third being an option. It was your typical alpine lodge, flower boxes on the balcony, mountain views all round. The interior had an interesting vintage feel to it, and the staff were friendly and welcoming again.

This was the first time I'd noticed the heavier German influence as the bilingual norm since entering South Tyrol. Road signs, locals and most in-store signage were German, a legacy of the region's history: it was part of Austria-Hungary until 1919, when it was ceded to Italy after the First World War under the Treaty of Saint-Germain.

Heading out from Corvara, I had a planned stop in Ortisei to visit Alpe di Siusi on the way through. The cable car cost €41, and I managed to find street parking in the blue bays for €7.50 for 3 hours. The surrounding car parks, including Alpe di Siusi's own, are a lot dearer, €20 cash only at their car park, so factor that in if convenience is your preference, though it does fill up quickly. The alpine plateau is beautiful once you reach the summit of Mont Sëuc at around 2,200m, and it's the largest high-altitude alpine meadow in Europe at around 56km². It's absolutely worth the visit, but it does mean another cable car to get up there. There are a number of walks you can do at the summit, but I opted to hike down to the bottom near the hotel and take a steady walk back, given I was on a time limit with a 3 hour drive to Anterselva still ahead of me. Once I reached the hotel I checked in, sorted a few bits out, then headed down for dinner and called it a night with an early start planned for the morning.

Day five was a day trip to Tre Cime national park. My toll road ticket cost €40 and was booked in advance. Toll slots seem to release 2-4 weeks ahead, but check your desired time and day regularly, as they sell fast. If you're hiring a car, you can buy a ticket and add the registration once you've collected it, though it needs updating on the reservation the day before to avoid issues at the gate. The toll gate signal was non-existent, so keep the ticket loaded or your email handy in case of trouble at the gate, though in practice they read my plate and I was through without issues. My ticket granted access from 9:30am for 12 hours. Make sure you've left before your exit time, or you'll likely get a fine, which isn't worth risking. Rifugios here don't seem to have cash machines either, so plan accordingly.

I did the loop around the three peaks, and given I'm not a hugely experienced hiker and not great with heights, it was still a good route for me. There are some steep sections both down and up, including narrow stretches with a drop to the side, but taking it slowly makes it manageable. The full loop, including stops at all the rifugios, was around 6.5 miles with roughly 440m of elevation gain. Some sections underfoot are loose, so proper footwear helps, and poles add stability. I left the park around 4:30pm and headed back to Anterselva for dinner and an early night.

Day six I headed to Lago di Braies in the morning. It was an odd start, heavy rain looked to be on the way but never actually arrived. I decided to get going and beat the tour groups, reaching the car park around 9am. It was fairly busy by then but nothing excessive. The lake was pretty, but the postcard shots you see on social media are practically impossible unless you're there at first light. Low cloud hung around that morning too, so it wasn't the usual shot, though it added some drama and atmosphere to the frame. Boat hire is around €50 if you want to do it, but the queue at this time of day was long, so expect a wait. There's a short walk around the lake with a bit of an incline, nothing strenuous, giving a few different perspectives. If I'm honest, this felt overhyped. There were people posing for photos everywhere you looked, and it left me underwhelmed. On top of that, parking at lot 3 cost €12 for under 3 hours, climbing to around €18 if you go over, which feels steep given how many people are funnelled through.

Around midday I headed to Prato Piazza. Note that there's a road access curfew between 10am and 3pm, when the plateau can only be reached on foot, by bike or by bus. The bus cost €4 each way, cash only, and parking for the day was €6. Not realising I had a 1 hour 40 minute wait for the next bus, I decided to hike up instead, especially after seeing a family make the climb with a dog and a dad pushing a buggy. The signposted time to the summit is around 1 hour 47 minutes, covering a 540m climb over roughly 2.5 miles, so it's fairly steep with no real breaks. If the dad made it to the top with the buggy, reasonable fitness should get most people up there too. There are cows roaming around throughout the climb, including on the road where it crosses, but they're harmless, so there's no need to worry if you come across any. I made it up in around 1 hour 10 minutes, beating the bus.

At the top I walked along the plateau to Ristorante Vallandro, where Monte Cristallo comes into view. There was some cloud around the summit unfortunately, but it was still worth the hike for the jagged outline of the peak. I stopped at Malga Prato Piazza rifugio for a drink and a toilet break before heading back down the same route to the lower car park. One thing to flag: the hiking route eventually joins the road as part of the one-way traffic light system, but it isn't clearly signposted from that point. If you've got no mobile signal to check, like I didn't, it's easy to miss. The hike overall for this region came to around 8.5 miles, including the elevation gain.

Day seven I had planned a trip to Val di Funes. I'd already dropped this stop twice on the itinerary but had an itch to go and shoot the mountain range rather than the church itself, which was the more interesting subject from my perspective, those dramatic jagged peaks in the distance. Sadly the weather had other ideas. After checking three independent webcams covering different viewpoints of the range hourly, the cloud thickened over them as the day went on before breaking into a heavy shower. Around 10:30am I abandoned the plan for a third time and headed to the biathlon Olympic stadium instead, a short 15-minute drive from the hotel.

Parking cost €10 for the day, which by this point I'd just accepted as the price of seeing different places. There were groups of skiers training on roller-blade-style skis built for the summer season, which made for an interesting stop, along with the Olympic symbols and banners still dotted around from earlier in the year.

Next to the stadium is Lago di Anterselva. This was genuinely impressive, and reminded me of some of the lakes I'd visited in New Zealand. Unlike Braies, it's fed by the Vedrette di Ries glaciers to the northwest, which gives it that turquoise-blue colour, especially when the sun catches it. I walked the loop around the lake, around 2.2 miles with minimal elevation gain, a welcome change from the previous hikes on the trip. I'd been on the fence about whether to bother with this one, but it was well worth the trip, especially given it was on the hotel's doorstep.

I headed back to the hotel around 3pm as the weather clouded over and the rain picked up through the afternoon. I packed up ready for departure the following day and got ready for my final dinner at the Brunnerhof.

Venice Day Trip

The final day I went down for breakfast just after 7am, having planned a day trip into Venice ahead of the flight home. I was on the road by around 8:30am and reached the rental company close to 12pm. I filled the car up and got it washed before dropping it back and catching the shuttle to the airport. There's a luggage storage company at Venice airport, costing €9 for the first 6 hours per bag and €0.90 an hour after that. I dropped my bag off by 1pm and caught the ATVO bus into Venice's Piazzale Roma, €20 return, arriving in the city by 1:40pm.

I'd loosely planned an itinerary knowing I wouldn't have much time and wanted to make the most of it. I headed to St Mark's Square for a wander, a string quartet playing to diners with the basilica in the background next to the Campanile. It was busy, and the narrow streets compressed the crowd enough to slow things down, which mattered given I was watching the clock to make my flight.

I stopped for gelato for the full Italian experience, then wandered through Cannaregio, noticeably quieter and with more of a local feel than the tourist hotspot of San Marco. I grabbed pizza, found a spot along the canal to sit, then headed back towards the bus station ready to catch my bus to the airport.

I didn't want to risk being late back, given traffic or bag desk issues, so I reached the airport around 6:40pm. With the ongoing complaints about security queues at major European airports, I didn't want to get caught out there either, so I built in plenty of time. The flight ended up delayed by over an hour, meaning I could technically have had an extra hour in Venice. Hindsight's a wonderful thing.

Rasun Anterselva Honest Take

Anterselva was a nice location, though slightly outside the Dolomites region itself. That said, everything for this stretch of the trip was within an hour, which made it more logistically and financially manageable than the Corvara stint. I did prefer being in the heart of the Dolomites though, given how dramatic the landscape was at every turn there.

Tre Cime, Prato Piazza and Lago di Anterselva all earned their place on the itinerary. Book your Tre Cime toll slot as early as possible to secure the date and time you want, they get taken fast. I'd honestly suggest skipping Braies unless you can get there before 7:30-8am to dodge as much of the crowd as possible.

The short Venice day trip was a good taster for whether a longer visit pays off, and in my opinion it does. It can be expensive, but there's also a more local, authentic side away from the tourist traps and tourist pricing, so it offers the best of both depending what you're after. I'd like to go back and properly visit the Basilica and the other attractions, and try either a gondola or a motoscafo, the traditional Venetian water taxi.

It's busy, popular and crowded, so that's something to expect and accept if you're visiting. It takes a while to move through certain areas, so if your time is tight I'd stick to the quieter north of the city or save it for another trip altogether.

Wrap Up

A Final Word On The Dolomites

The Dolomites is somewhere I'd wanted to visit for a few years, but never quite managed to time for a summer visit until this year, 2026. The region is genuinely stunning, delivering incredible landscapes at almost every turn, so if you're into that kind of scenery, or enjoy cycling, mountain biking or hiking, it's a big tick.

My only real downside is how expensive things were. It's an alpine region, so a premium is expected, but at times it felt unnecessarily so. Cable cars in particular stood out: in winter they grant access to a full day's skiing, but in summer you're only paying for access to the summit and nothing else, so summer lift prices should arguably come down to reflect that. Parking felt similarly steep, almost priced on the assumption tourists have no choice but to pay it. The extra costs at mountain hut meals and paid toilets added to that feeling too.

That said, I'd do it again, though next time I'd go back in winter for the contrast of snow-capped peaks against the same dramatic landscape. I'd stick to the central Dolomites region too. Being right in the thick of the mountains, the winding alpine roads and the switchbacks made it feel like the heart of the trip. Just go in expecting it to cost more than you'd think.
